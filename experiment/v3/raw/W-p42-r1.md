Drawing on decentralization as the lens here, specifically through a distributed-systems engineer's eye: the discipline that spends most of its time on exactly your failure pattern, a fragile node that quietly became load-bearing for something it never needed to be.

Three things surfaced that seem worth taking home.

**1. The wall switch shouldn't need the brain to work.**

In distributed systems, the failure that actually hurts users isn't "a node went down," it's "a function that could have run locally got routed through the node that went down." That's precisely what's happening to your bedroom lights: basic on/off has been made to depend on the same fragile box that also runs your Zigbee coordinator, MQTT broker, and Node-RED. When the SD card corrupts, a control-plane failure (the Pi) is taking down a data-plane function (switch talks to bulb) that never needed the brain in the first place.

Concrete move: bind the switch to the bulb directly at the Zigbee level, a group or device binding set up once in Zigbee2MQTT's or deCONZ's binding UI, not a Node-RED flow. That link lives in the Zigbee devices themselves, so basic toggle survives a dead Pi. Keep Node-RED for the things that legitimately need judgment (schedules, sensor-triggered scenes), not for the one function your partner actually depends on daily.

**2. Your backup should be a habit, not a snapshot.**

A distributed version-control system doesn't protect you by keeping many copies of the code around; it protects you by making every commit a small, durable, checkable record of a decision, which is exactly the thing you're currently trying to reconstruct from memory. Your two corruptions this year have cost you twice: the running system, and the record of why any given flow does what it does. A year-old full disk image is the pattern this replaces: one monolithic, staling copy instead of a running trail of small ones.

Concrete move: put flows.json and any custom function-node code in a small git repo, and adopt one habit, commit at the end of every session with a one-line message on what changed and why, even a fifteen-minute session. A few months of that and `git log` is a better memory of your own automations than you are. Recovery after the next corruption becomes reflash-and-clone instead of rebuild-from-memory, and you get a real answer to "wait, why did I write it this way" for the flows you no longer recognize.

**3. Something is actively wearing out that card, and it's probably not your automations.**

In signal/noise terms, what physically kills a flash medium is rarely the signal you meant to write, it's the noise: logs, retained MQTT messages, churny state files, written continuously by processes with no idea they're eroding the substrate underneath. Two corruptions in a year on a Pi 4 running Zigbee2MQTT and Node-RED has a very typical cause: those two processes write constantly (MQTT retained-message churn, Node-RED's context store, Zigbee2MQTT's state.json and logs), and it's almost certainly that traffic grinding the card down, not the handful of flows you wrote two years ago.

Concrete move: move Node-RED's userDir/context storage and Zigbee2MQTT's log and state files onto a cheap USB SSD (or tmpfs for the pure log output), leaving the SD card holding only the OS and your now-git-tracked flow files, read-mostly, which is the write pattern SD cards actually tolerate. This is the piece that should stop the corruption from recurring at all, rather than just making the next one cheaper to recover from.

Given four hours a weekend: the Zigbee binding (1) is a single-session fix and buys your partner immediate peace of mind. Git-tracking flows.json (2) is a five-minute setup plus a habit. Moving storage off the SD card (3) is the one real project, probably two sessions.
