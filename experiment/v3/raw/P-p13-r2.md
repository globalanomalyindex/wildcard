This is a good problem to think laterally about — a few angles that sit outside typical dashboard-design thinking:

**1. Air traffic control displays solved this exact fight decades ago.** ATC screens pack enormous state into small radar scopes not through smaller text, but through *encoding urgency in visual weight* — position and motion carry the routine information, while color/blink/shape are reserved almost exclusively for exceptions. Your veterans aren't actually reading 18 columns of text fluently — they're pattern-matching on shape and color drift, and the text is just the fallback when something looks wrong. Worth trying: instrument which cells veterans' eyes actually land on before they act (even informally, by asking a few to narrate their scan), because the real information density they need might be far lower than 18 columns implies. The table may be a security blanket, not the interface they're actually using.

**2. This looks like a font-hinting problem wearing a UX costume.** Type designers solved "must be legible at tiny sizes on real screens" long before web dashboards existed — that's literally what hinting, and later fonts like Verdana or the "tabular figures" feature in modern typefaces, were built for. Most dashboard fonts are optimized for prose, not for dense numeric/alpha scanning at 11px. Worth trying: before touching layout, test whether a screen-optimized or tabular-numeral font alone (not a redesign) shrinks the "three months to fluency" and the eye-strain complaints. It's possible you're fighting a layout battle that's actually a typography battle.

**3. Reframe "small fleets" and "veteran dispatchers" as two different users of two different products, not one screen failing two audiences.** Flight-booking sites do this constantly — Kayak shows dense grids to power users while a simplified "explore" mode exists for casual searchers, same data, different entry point, and nobody feels patronized because the dense view was always one click away, never removed. Your prototype "failed" because it replaced the veteran view instead of sitting *beside* it. Worth trying: pitch the roomier layout purely as a sales-demo and onboarding mode (with an obvious "switch to expert view" toggle) rather than a replacement — this also directly kills the sales-demo problem without asking veterans to give up anything.

**4. Ask what the *aging workforce* actually needs, because it may not be size — it might be contrast and update-rate, not point size.** Low-vision and ergonomics research (think e-reader design, or cockpit displays for older pilots) shows a lot of "eye strain" complaints trace to contrast ratio, flicker/refresh, and font weight rather than raw character size — people compensate for small-but-crisp text fine, but small-and-low-contrast is exhausting. Worth trying: an audit of your color contrast ratios and whether the grid re-renders/flickers on updates, separate from the font-size conversation — you might buy real relief without touching the 18-column layout at all, which sidesteps the veteran revolt entirely.
