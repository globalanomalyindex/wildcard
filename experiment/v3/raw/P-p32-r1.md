Here are a few angles from outside the usual "bakery ops" frame:

**1. Hospital OR block scheduling**
Hospitals face the same fight you have between bread and laminated dough: multiple teams needing the same limited rooms/equipment, with demand that shifts daily. Their fix isn't smarter daily negotiation — it's fixed weekly "block time": Tuesday mornings belong to ortho, period, whether or not ortho has a full slate that day. You might try assigning oven and cold-room hours to bread vs. laminated dough by day-of-week in advance, rather than re-deciding the tradeoff every single night. It trades some flexibility for the thing you're actually short on: a schedule nobody has to hold in their head.

**2. Airline standby/cutoff tiers**
Airlines take bookings for months but still get last-minute changes, and they don't let 9pm chaos rewrite the whole manifest. They split demand into "confirmed" (locked in early, guaranteed a seat) and "standby" (late requests filled only from a small reserved buffer, not by reshuffling everything). You could try a hard order cutoff — say 6 or 7pm — after which cafes are quoting against a small pre-built flex batch, not renegotiating your night's production. This could be the actual lever for both the waste and the shortage complaints: overproduce a small standby pool sized to the two chronically-shorting cafes' actual variance, rather than an across-the-board 8% buffer.

**3. Ant colony "stigmergy"**
Ant colonies coordinate complex logistics with no individual ant holding the plan — they leave trail markers in the environment (pheromones) so the *environment* carries the memory, not any one ant's brain. Your head baker is a single point of failure by design: the schedule lives only in him, so cutting his nights feels dangerous rather than just operationally different. Worth trying: externalize the schedule onto something the whole shift can read and mark up (a whiteboard grid of oven/cold-room slots by hour, a shared doc) so the knowledge survives him stepping back — which is likely a precondition for him actually getting to four nights, not just a nice-to-have.

**4. Theory of Constraints / "protect the bottleneck"**
In manufacturing, when you have one scarce resource (your two ovens + cold room), the rule is: don't let anything outside the bottleneck dictate its schedule — build a time buffer *around* the constraint and make everything else subordinate to it. Right now your bottleneck's schedule is effectively being set by whoever texts last at 9:58pm. You might try flipping the causality: decide the oven/cold-room plan first, then treat late orders as requests to fit into remaining buffer, not as edits the constraint has to absorb.
