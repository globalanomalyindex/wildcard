Drawing on embroidery, materials lens, here's where it led.

Picture a working embroiderer, someone who thinks constantly in thread, stabilizer, dye lot, and the reverse side of the cloth. Three things surfaced that seem worth handing over.

**1. Dye lots and anchor knots, for the traceability gap**

Embroidery floss comes in dye lots: two skeins labeled the same color can differ just enough that a trained eye catches it, and once both are stitched into the cloth you can no longer sort them apart. So needleworkers write the lot number on the skein wrapper the moment they open it, before it ever touches the fabric, and knot off at intervals so a broken thread means retracing one short stretch, not the whole line.

Your two suppliers feeding one dock work the same way: once product from both lots lands on the same tray during the morning sort, it's visually indistinguishable, same as unmarked floss.

Concrete move: tag every crate with a lot and catch-time code the moment it's landed, before it's sorted, not after. Then treat each transfer point (landing, sort, loading onto the delivery van) as a checkpoint where that code gets scanned or logged. A chef's rejection becomes a lookup against the nearest checkpoint instead of an afternoon of paper invoices.

**2. The back of the work, for the dock excursions**

Distortion in embroidery never happens while the fabric is held taut in the hoop. It happens in the unsupported stretch between hoopings, and that's exactly the stretch a careful embroiderer checks by flipping the piece over, because tension problems show on the back before they ever show on the front.

Your reefer vans are the hoop, held taut, temperature fine. The morning sort is the unsupported stretch: no equivalent temperature envelope, and like the back of the piece, nobody's watching it directly. You find out from the front, a chef's rejection, or this month, three logged excursions after the fact.

Concrete move: a continuous logging temperature sensor at the dock staging area specifically (separate from the van units, which are already fine), with an alarm set at 40 degrees. One device gets you the inspector's records automatically and a live catch the moment an excursion starts, instead of finding out at the seam.

**3. Remnant triage, for the day-two write-off**

A partly used skein doesn't get forced back into the big project, that risks a visible color mismatch. It gets consciously downgraded, right away, to small motifs or sold as remnant stock, decided the moment it's clear the skein can't serve its original purpose, not after it's sat in the stash degrading further.

Your unsold inventory currently runs on a single cliff: full price through day two, then dumped to the fish market at a loss. That's closer to letting the skein sit undecided until forced.

Concrete move: build a tiered schedule instead of one cliff, say a modest discount partway through day two to move product into secondary channels early (a second-tier restaurant list, a standing wholesale buyer above fish-market price), with the fish market only as the true last resort. Deciding the tier earlier, the way the remnant gets sorted the moment it's clear it won't serve the original order, should recover more of the margin you're currently losing at the cliff.
