Here are a few lateral angles — none of these replace the standard postgres playbook (partition, tune autovacuum, add a replica), but they might reframe *why* those moves work or suggest a shape for the migration itself.

**1. Treat the events table like a log-segment store, not a table.**
What you're describing — one huge append-only stream that gets read in recent time-windows and never touched further back — is structurally the same problem Kafka, logrotate, and LSM-tree storage engines solve. Their answer is never "clean the whole log," it's "close old segments and stop touching them." Postgres native partitioning by time range is the direct analog: each partition becomes a closed segment that autovacuum can mostly ignore once it's cold, and your 90-day query stops being a full scan and becomes "touch ~90 partitions, prune the rest." The mental shift that might help: you're not vacuuming a table, you're managing segment lifecycle.

**2. The autovacuum lag might be a garbage-collector generational problem, not a scheduling problem.**
JVM/GC tuning has a well-worn insight: "most objects die young," so generational collectors focus effort on young objects and rarely sweep old ones. Your table is append-heavy, so the "garbage" isn't dead tuples from updates — it's mostly the freeze/wraparound work autovacuum has to do on every row eventually. Postgres 13 actually added insert-driven autovacuum triggers (`autovacuum_vacuum_insert_scale_factor`) for exactly this pattern, but they're rarely tuned by default. Worth trying: tune those thresholds so freezing happens continuously in small bites (like a prescribed burn) instead of autovacuum falling behind and eventually having to do a huge catch-up pass during business hours.

**3. Borrow the "shadow table" cutover pattern from online schema-change tools.**
Tools like gh-ost and pt-online-schema-change exist because MySQL shops had the same constraint you have: can't take downtime, can't hand-edit the app. Their trick is build a new correctly-shaped table alongside the old one, dual-write or replicate into it, then do an atomic rename at the very end — turning a multi-hour blocking migration into a few seconds of cutover. You could do the postgres-native version of this with logical replication: build a partitioned replacement table, backfill and replicate live, and use your Sunday window only for the final swap rather than for the whole migration. This decouples "the risky part" from "the scarce time window."

**4. The EU-morning/nightly-job collision might be a demand-response problem, not a query-tuning problem.**
Rather than trying to make the reporting job or the aggregation queries individually faster, consider treating this like a power grid managing peak load: shift the *category* of work off the primary entirely. A logical-replication read replica dedicated to reporting is the direct analog of demand response — the OLTP primary never sees the reporting job's I/O at all, so the collision with EU peak traffic stops being a timing puzzle to solve and just goes away structurally. This also sidesteps the ORM constraint, since the replica can be queried directly by the reporting job without touching app code.
