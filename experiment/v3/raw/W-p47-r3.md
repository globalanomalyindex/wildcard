Drawing on a corner of neonatal nursing that's about as far from a family archive as you can get, but the rhythm turned out to travel.

I'm a NICU nurse who specializes in humidity weaning for micro-preemies — the 22-to-26-weekers whose skin hasn't finished keratinizing yet. My whole job is pulling an isolette down from 80-85% humidity without hurting the baby: too fast, and transepidermal water loss spikes their sodium; too slow, and the same warm wet air that's protecting them starts breeding Candida in the skin folds. Nothing I do is a single event on a calendar. It's a stepped wean — drop the setting, hold, check the numbers, then either take the next step or bump back up.

Three things from that rhythm that might be worth something to you:

**1. The birth-year conflict — and the two notebooks and Word doc behind it.**
In my unit, a humidity change that isn't charted with the vitals that justified it doesn't count — it's just a number someone remembers changing. Your birth-year dispute is stuck for the same reason: the value survived (in both your tree and your uncle's GEDCOM), but the "why" behind each one didn't. You likely can't resolve which year is right without new evidence, but you can stop losing this ability going forward. Make one flowsheet — a single spreadsheet, one row per contested fact — with columns Fact | Value | Source | Date entered | Confidence. Retroactively fill in whatever you can reconstruct for the great-grandmother entry, even if it's just "unknown which source, contradicts GEDCOM." Then require every new fact you add to your 312-person tree to carry a source before it goes in. No exceptions, the way I wouldn't chart a setting without the reading that justified it.

**2. The $240 renewal — treat it as a wean, not a cliff.**
I never pull an isolette straight to open air. I step humidity down in increments, usually 5-10 points every 24-48 hours, and I only advance to the next step once the numbers confirm the last one was tolerated — if they're not, I go back up, because a reversible pause beats a hard cutoff you can't undo. You've got roughly four weeks before that subscription lapses. Instead of deciding renew-or-cancel as one binary choice at the deadline, spend this month exporting in stages while you still have full access: week 1, pull the full GEDCOM/record export for all 312 people plus every source document attached to the disputed birth-year entry; week 2, resolve or discard your pending hints/match queue; week 3, download full-resolution copies of any Polish church record still cited only by a link rather than a saved file. At each week's checkpoint, actually open the exported files and confirm they're legible — if a step fails (a broken export, a dead source link), don't advance to cancellation; most services let you pause rather than cancel outright, which keeps the door open the way stepping humidity back up does.

**3. The shoebox — and the relatives in their eighties.**
Part of why we wean as soon as the skin shows real keratinization, rather than waiting for a more convenient point, is that leaving fragile skin in a warm wet environment longer than it needs raises infection risk on its own — the window costs you from both directions, not just one. Your photo situation has the same shape: waiting risks losing the people who can identify them, but one marathon "help me go through this whole shoebox" session risks losing the visit to fatigue before much gets done. Structure it like a paced session instead of a single big ask: number the back of 10-12 photos in advance, bring only those to each visit, cap it at 20-30 minutes, and ask one specific question per photo — who is this, and how old were you — rather than open sorting. Repeatable short sittings will get you further than one long one.
