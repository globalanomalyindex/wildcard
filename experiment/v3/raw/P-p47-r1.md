A few sideways angles, take what's useful and leave the rest:

**1. Bezos's "one-way door vs. two-way door" framing**
What I noticed: you're carrying three kinds of pressure (subscription deadline, elderly relatives, messy notes) as if they're equally urgent, but they're not the same *kind* of urgent. A subscription lapsing is a two-way door — you can always resubscribe, and the data doesn't vanish, it just goes read-only or gets exported. Relatives in their eighties who could identify a face in a photo are a one-way door — once that's gone, no amount of money or effort reopens it. Worth trying: let the door-type set your order of operations, not the calendar. Spend this month's attention on the irreversible thing (a photo-ID session with relatives, even informal, even recorded on a phone) and treat the subscription as the thing you're allowed to solve less elegantly, or later, or by just exporting what you need before it lapses.

**2. Chain of custody, borrowed from forensics/museum provenance work**
What I noticed: the birth-year conflict isn't really a *research* problem anymore, it's a *provenance* problem — you have two claims and no record of which evidence backs which. Forensic and museum practice solves this not by re-investigating from scratch but by refusing to record any fact without also recording its source, so conflicts become visible and traceable instead of silently merging into "just what the tree says." Worth trying: before doing any new research, go back through what you already have and tag each disputed fact with its source (which document, which tree, your uncle's tree vs. yours). You may not resolve which year is correct, but you'll at least stop losing the ability to tell.

**3. Git merge conflicts, from version control**
What I noticed: you have two trees that disagree, which is structurally identical to two branches of code that diverged and now need merging — and the software world's answer isn't "pick one and discard the other," it's to look at the diff line by line and keep provenance for each choice. Worth trying: instead of trying to reconcile the two GEDCOMs into one "true" tree in your head, treat your uncle's tree as a separate branch and do a field-by-field diff — for each disagreement, note both values and which source (if any) supports each, rather than silently overwriting one with the other. Some genealogy tools support this kind of "conflicting fact" annotation natively.

**4. Journalism's "capture now, verify later"**
What I noticed: you're implicitly treating identification and preservation as one step ("figure out who's in the photo, then it's documented"), but reporters interviewing sources under time pressure split those into two separate acts on purpose — record everything raw first, fact-check afterward, because the capture window and the verification window close at different speeds. Worth trying: sit with a relative and just get them talking over the photos, recorded, without pausing to cross-check dates or spell names correctly in the moment. A messy transcript with "I think that's Aunt Zosia" is far more recoverable later than a lost afternoon spent trying to get everything precisely right the first time.
