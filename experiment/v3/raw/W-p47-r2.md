**A voice from outside the problem:**

"I engrave narrative friezes into chalcedony cylinder seals, and just as much of my working life goes into measuring them afterward — drill-bore diameter, cutting-wheel groove width, wear-polish gradients — because almost none of my seals carry their own date. A seal that looks ancient but drills a uniform modern bore is a forgery. A seal whose second half of the register shows crisper tool marks than the first has been recut and rededicated by a later owner. What I fear most isn't a clumsy image — it's a beautiful, *unprovenanced* piece: no recorded findspot, nothing independently dated to check it against, so I can't anchor it no matter how fine the carving."

A few things that genuinely carried over into your problem:

**1. Go looking for the buried findspot before you go looking for new evidence.**
An object without a recorded findspot can't anchor a date, however fine the work — so before anything else, I go hunting for whatever fragment of context still clings to it. Your birth-year conflict is the same shape: you don't lack sources, you lack the *link* between claim and source, and that link may just be buried, not gone. Open both GEDCOM files — yours and your uncle's — in a plain text editor instead of the tree viewer. GEDCOM stores citations under `SOUR` tags and free text under `NOTE` tags per individual, and most tree-viewer UIs collapse or hide them. Search both files for your great-grandmother's name and read the raw `SOUR`/`NOTE` blocks around each birth-year entry. While you're in there, compare the `CHAN` (change-date) tag on her record in each file — if one looks like an edited copy of the other (same facts, one field changed, a later `CHAN` date), you're not reconciling two independent witnesses, you're finding one edit that needs undoing.

**2. Don't trust the self-declared date — check it against the one source that can't be edited after the fact.**
I never take a seal's own claimed date at face value; I check it against exemplars whose date comes from outside the object, usually a stratified excavation context. Both your trees are self-declared claims — yours and your uncle's, either of which could carry an unflagged typo or a guess hardened into fact over time. The church records are your independent anchor, and you don't need to read Polish to use them. You don't need the shoebox translated wholesale: narrow to the parish and the specific year(s) in dispute, pull just those birth/baptism entries, and get only those 1-2 pages translated. A genealogy-record translator or a volunteer through FamilySearch's Polish-records community can usually turn that around cheaply, since these entries follow a fixed, formulaic layout even to someone reading them cold.

**3. Spend your urgency budget on what's closing, not on what's merely unfinished.**
When I get called to a site before it's built over, I don't deep-study any single piece first — I do one fast measurement pass across the whole hoard, because the site's information disappears in weeks and my lab time isn't the scarce resource. Right now you have exactly one closing-window resource in this whole project, and it isn't the trees or the records — it's the relatives who can still name the faces in that shoebox. Before more reconciliation work, run one fast session with them: don't try to fully verify each photo, just log a name and rough decade per photo as you go, tentative attributions included, written down immediately even when unsure. The birth-year conflict, the $240 renewal, the two notebooks — all of that can wait on your own schedule. The photo IDs can't.
