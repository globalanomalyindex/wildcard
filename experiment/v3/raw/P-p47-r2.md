A few outside-the-frame angles that might reshuffle how you're looking at this:

**1. Treat the conflicting birth years like a merge conflict, not a mystery to solve.**
What you noticed: you have two trees that disagree, and you've lost the thread of which source backs which claim. That's not really a genealogy problem, it's a version-control problem. In software, when two branches disagree, nobody tries to guess which one is "right" from memory. They diff them and tag every claim with its origin.
Connection: you don't need to resolve the birth year discrepancy right now. You need a one-line "commit log" for each fact: birth year A, from [uncle's GEDCOM, source unknown], birth year B, from [church record, page X]. Once every claim has a citation attached, the conflict becomes trivial to resolve later, or fine to leave open with both sources flagged.
Something to try: before touching anything else, spend 20 minutes just re-attaching known sources to the two conflicting dates. You don't have to solve it today, just stop it from getting more tangled.

**2. Sort your whole to-do list by perishability, not importance, like an ER triage nurse would.**
What you noticed: some of what you have will still exist in five years exactly as it is now (the digital tree, the subscription, the notebooks). Some of it is actively decaying (memories in relatives in their 80s, unlabeled photos nobody will be able to identify once those relatives are gone).
Connection: triage doesn't ask "what matters most," it asks "what disappears if I don't act in the next window." Your subscription and your two trees are recoverable at any time, you can always re-pay $240 or re-derive dates from records. A relative's memory of who's in a 1940s photo is not recoverable once it's gone.
Something to try: make one list of "can wait indefinitely" (tree merging, notebook consolidation, subscription decision) and one list of "closes forever" (photo ID sessions with the elderly relatives). Let the second list jump the queue, even if it feels less "organized" to do it out of order.

**3. Borrow the oral-history interview format instead of just showing relatives photos and asking "who's this."**
What you noticed: you're treating your elderly relatives as a lookup table (name the face), which is a slow, exhausting way to extract information and tends to produce short, low-context answers.
Connection: oral history projects like StoryCorps don't ask "who is this," they ask people to narrate around an object, "tell me about a summer you remember from that house," while a photo or document sits in front of them, and record the whole conversation. The tangents usually contain more genealogical gold (nicknames, feuds, migration stories, who-married-whom) than the direct question ever would.
Something to try: bring the shoebox to one relative, hit record on your phone, and just let them talk through the photos in whatever order they want. You can transcribe and mine it later. A recorded ramble beats a rushed labeling session.

**4. Treat the Polish church records like formulaic legal documents, not a language you need to learn.**
What you noticed: you're stuck because you can't read Polish, which makes the records feel like a wall.
Connection: parish records from this era (and their Latin-influenced predecessors) follow rigid templates, baptism, marriage, and death entries repeat the same handful of phrases and structure every single time. This is closer to reading a legal form than reading a novel, paralegals learn to parse contracts they don't fully "speak" by pattern-matching the recurring scaffolding, not by mastering the language.
Something to try: instead of translating the records, look up a genealogy-specific glossary of the ~30 recurring Polish/Latin terms used in these documents (there are free ones built exactly for this). You'll likely be able to extract names, dates, and relationships without ever "reading Polish."

All optional, obviously, take whichever (if any) actually fits how you work.
