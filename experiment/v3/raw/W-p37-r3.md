I spent a few minutes as the person who minds malaxation temperature at a small-batch olive press: the 30-45 minute stage after crushing where paste gets slowly churned so the oil droplets coalesce before pressing. My whole job is watching paste temperature, because the paste heats itself — friction off the beaters, enzyme activity — faster than any water jacket can compensate for, and a jacket set-point is never the same number as what the paste is actually doing at the wall.

Three things from that vat that might be useful here:

**1. Measure at the failure site, not the flow site.**
In my vat, the reading that matters is paste temperature right at the blades, not the jacket gauge — because that's where local overheating actually happens, and a center probe or jacket reading can look fine while the paste at the friction zone has already run hot. Your reefer vans read fine for the same reason: that's where you're measuring. The excursions are happening at the dock during the morning sort, the one segment with no instrument on it at all. Put a continuous logger — the ~$30-60 class of device millers use for cold-extraction certification (Testo, LogTag, similar) — physically on the dock sort table, logging every 1-2 minutes from truck-open to van-load. That's the literal continuous record the inspector is asking for, and it's the first data that would tell you whether all three excursions this month happened at the same point in the sort or scattered.

**2. Stamp the lot at the point of no return, not after.**
Once two lots of paste mix in my vat I can't un-mix them, so lot ID gets written down the moment a delivery crosses into the mill, never reconstructed afterward. Your traceability problem lives entirely in the "afterward" — the lot data sits on paper invoices, decoupled from the box, so a chef rejection means reconstructing after the fact what should never have separated from the product. During the sort you're already running, put a sticker on each box — supplier, landing date, a short sort-batch code — right when it crosses the dock. A rejection then becomes "read the sticker," not an afternoon in the invoice folder.

**3. Let the dose gate the deadline, not the calendar.**
Malaxation quality isn't gated by a clock, it's gated by a joint time-and-temperature dose: paste that ran a few degrees hot for 40 minutes needs to move to the press sooner than paste that stayed cool the whole time, even though both got "the same" malaxation time. Your day-2 discount rule is a flat clock applied to every box regardless of its actual cold-chain history — a box with zero excursions and one that sat through all three this month get treated identically. Once the dock log from #1 exists, you could split day-2 handling on it: zero-excursion boxes get a slightly longer window before the fish-market discount, any box that touched a logged excursion gets flagged for same-day sale or write-off before day 2. One blind deadline becomes two, driven by logged minutes above 40°F instead of the calendar.
