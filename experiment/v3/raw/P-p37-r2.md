Here are a few angles from fields that deal with similar bottlenecks, just wearing different clothes.

**1. Hospital ER "boarding" problem**
What I noticed: your dock is doing exactly what an ER waiting room does — patients (boxes) arrive faster than they can be triaged into beds (routes), so they sit in the hallway accumulating risk. Hospitals that fixed this didn't buy more ambulances; they built real-time bed-status boards and re-sequenced triage so the most time-critical cases get processed first, not first-come-first-served. Worth trying: instead of sorting by whatever order boxes come off the truck, triage by "time since landing" or shelf-life-remaining the moment they hit the dock, so the most perishable lots get sorted and moved out first, not last.

**2. Airline baggage chain-of-custody**
What I noticed: airlines solved "which bag is this and where has it been" cheaply, with barcode tags scanned at every handoff point, not with expensive sensors on every suitcase. That's basically your traceability problem. Worth trying: a $20 barcode/QR label per lot at intake, scanned at receiving, at sort, and at loadout with a phone app — three scans gets you full chain of custody without digging through paper invoices, and it's a much smaller lift than a full track-and-trace system.

**3. Blood bank cold-chain indicators**
What I noticed: blood products have the same legal temperature-record requirement you're facing, and blood banks often solve it with cheap irreversible time-temperature indicator stickers (they change color permanently once a threshold is crossed) rather than continuous digital logging. Worth trying: a $0.50-$1 indicator sticker per case that visually shows "this excursion happened" gives you both the inspector-facing record and an instant reject/accept signal for the chef, without instrumenting the whole dock.

**4. Restaurant expo line discipline**
What I noticed: your morning sort is structurally the same as a restaurant's plating line under a heat lamp, where dishes degrade the longer they sit waiting for expo to call them. Kitchens solve this with physical FIFO staging (first ordered, first fired, first out) rather than sorting by whatever's easiest to reach. Worth trying: physically stage the dock by "landed earliest" instead of by supplier or destination, so the oldest product is structurally forced to the front of the sort rather than relying on someone remembering to prioritize it.

Any of these might just be a nudge toward the same fix (label at intake, triage by time-critical first) approached from a different industry's already-solved version of your problem.
