Here are a few angles that might be useful, pulled from fields that deal with similar structural problems even though they're not about moving at all:

**1. Blue-green deployment (software release engineering)**
What I noticed: you're treating the six-day lease overlap as a scheduling headache, but it's structurally identical to what's called a "blue-green deploy" — running old and new environments simultaneously so a cutover happens with zero downtime, instead of a hard switch.
The connection: engineers don't try to compress the overlap, they exploit it — deliberately doing the riskiest transfers *during* the window when both environments are live, and treating "old apartment still works" as a rollback option.
Worth trying: use the six days to get the ISP installed at the new place (if the backlog clock can start now) while your current internet still works as backup, and sequence movable non-essentials first, leaving the last day for anything that breaks.

**2. Message broker pattern (distributed systems)**
What I noticed: you're mentally modeling this as "40 separate updates," but the passport-in-transit issue and the 40-accounts issue are actually one problem — information routing, not 40 problems.
The connection: instead of 40 point-to-point calls, insert one relay in the middle — USPS mail forwarding (not just a hold) acts like a message broker, catching anything addressed to the old place and rerouting it, so accounts you can't get to in five weeks aren't silently lost.
Worth trying: file the forwarding first, before anything else — it converts "40 urgent updates" into "40 updates on whatever timeline I want," since misdirected mail no longer means missed mail.

**3. ER triage, not a to-do list**
What I noticed: "forty accounts" is being carried as one undifferentiated mass, which is exactly the kind of load that causes paralysis.
The connection: triage nurses don't sort by how many patients there are, they sort by what happens if this one waits. Your 40 have wildly different failure modes — a passport mid-mail is time-critical, voter registration has essentially no consequence for weeks, car registration usually has a legal grace period, utilities are date-locked to the move.
Worth trying: sort into "breaks if delayed a day" / "breaks if delayed a month" / "no real deadline" — probably shrinks the *actually urgent during business hours* pile from 40 down to a handful.

**4. Pit crew handoffs, not personal presence**
What I noticed: the real constraint isn't 40 tasks, it's that synchronous tasks (phone calls) are colliding with a fixed 9-6 job — a scheduling collision, not a task-volume problem.
The connection: pit crews solve "everything must happen in a narrow window with one person" by pre-assigning who touches what, rather than one person doing it all sequentially in real time.
Worth trying: for the specific offices that share your hours, look for anything asynchronous they offer (chat, portal, scheduled callback) before assuming it needs a live call — and consider whether the new building's leasing office can act as your on-site proxy for the ISP install, since that's the one appointment that truly can't be squeezed into your day.
