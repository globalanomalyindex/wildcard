A few angles from outside the genealogy-research frame that might loosen this up:

**1. Triage, not to-do list (emergency medicine framing)**
What you noticed: every item on your plate feels urgent, but they're not equally urgent, they're differently *reversible*. A subscription can be re-bought. A GEDCOM conflict can sit unresolved for another year with zero new damage. But a memory in an 88-year-old's head is the one asset on your list that is actually expiring.
How it connects: ER triage doesn't ask "what's most upsetting," it asks "what gets worse if I wait." Applied here, only one category clears that bar.
What you might try: before touching the trees or the notebooks, do a single afternoon with the photos and the relative(s) who can still ID them, phone recording, no research agenda, just "who's this." Everything else on your list is data. That's the only irreplaceable source.

**2. Two GEDCOMs, one merge conflict (version control framing)**
What you noticed: you're describing two trees that disagree on a fact, with the provenance for each claim lost, which is structurally identical to a git merge conflict where someone deleted the commit history.
How it connects: nobody resolves a merge conflict by re-reading both files from scratch and trying to remember which one they trust more. They diff them, field by field, and mark each conflict explicitly rather than letting it stay ambient anxiety.
What you might try: export both trees to GEDCOM/CSV and literally diff them (even a spreadsheet with two columns works), flag every disagreement, not just the birth year, you may find that's not the only one, and treat "unknown, conflicting sources" as a legitimate tagged status rather than something you have to solve before moving on.

**3. Photo elicitation (oral history / ethnography method)**
What you noticed: you're planning to ask relatives to identify photos, but the framing "who is this" is actually a weaker memory cue than researchers use on purpose.
How it connects: oral historians and ethnographers use "photo elicitation," showing an image and asking open questions ("where do you think this was taken," "what's she wearing," "who would have owned a camera then") because it triggers narrative and peripheral memory, not just a name-recall test that can fail and stall the whole conversation.
What you might try: instead of one flat pass through the shoebox, sort photos loosely first (by decade, by clothing, by setting) so the visit becomes a conversation with texture, not a quiz. You'll likely get names for photos you didn't even ask about directly.

**4. The Polish records aren't just a translation problem, they're a community problem**
What you noticed: you're treating "I can't read Polish" as a solo blocker to solve or pay for.
How it connects: historical-document transcription has an entire crowdsourced-citizen-science model behind it (the way Zooniverse gets volunteers to transcribe ship logs or census records), and Polish genealogy specifically has active volunteer translation communities used to exactly this kind of scanned-church-record handwriting.
What you might try: post the specific scans (the ones bearing on the birth-year conflict first) to a Polish genealogy translation group before spending on broader tools. You may get the actual disputed fact resolved for free, faster than any subscription would.
