# Four angles from outside product design

**1. Evolutionary biology — vestigial genes are conditionally expressed, not deleted**
What I noticed: your situation resembles a genome that's accumulated genes from many lineages (acquisitions) — you can't delete DNA that some cell type still needs, but that doesn't mean every cell expresses every gene. Liver cells and neurons share a genome but look completely different because gene *expression* is conditional on cell type, not on gene existence.
Connection: your nav is trying to be one "cell type" (one interface) expressing all genes (all 13 items) all the time, for everyone. The 60,000 accounts aren't one organism, they're many.
What to try: stop thinking of nav as a single artifact to prune, and start thinking of it as a genome with per-account expression rules. Every item stays in the codebase/architecture (nothing is deleted, so no escalation trigger), but what renders by default is computed per account from actual usage — the enterprise account using item #11 daily keeps seeing it; the new self-serve signup who's never touched it doesn't. This is a data/personalization problem, not a menu-redesign problem.

**2. Political science — federalism and subsidiarity, not one national law**
What I noticed: you're trying to govern 60,000 "jurisdictions" with a single national constitution, and every past deal effectively signed a side-treaty ("we need this in the nav") that you're now treating as permanent global law.
Connection: federalist systems solve this by keeping a thin layer of universal rights/items at the center and devolving everything else to the state/local level — local law doesn't require renegotiating the whole country, and citizens moving between states expect different menus of services.
What to try: version your nav like an API or a legal contract, per account, instead of as one global object everyone shares. Legacy enterprise accounts keep their "ratified treaty" (their current nav) indefinitely unless *they* choose to migrate — that alone removes the account-rep escalation risk, since you're never moving anyone's menu out from under them again. New signups get issued the current, minimal constitution by default. Bloat stops compounding because you stop forcing convergence.

**3. Urban planning — desire paths and the arterial/local road split**
What I noticed: cities with old cores facing the same problem (too many streets serving too few people well) don't demolish neighborhoods to fix traffic. They separate through-traffic infrastructure (highways, arterial roads — fast, minimal, for the common case) from local streets (numerous, specific, still fully accessible, just not the default route).
Connection: your complaint "can't find anything" is a wayfinding problem, not strictly an inventory problem — 13 things can coexist with an interface that *feels* like 4 if there's a clear "highway" for the 80% case.
What to try: build the equivalent of a command palette / universal search as the arterial road — the fast default path to any of the 13 items or 9 settings tabs, so most navigation never touches the visible top-level menu at all. Then let the visible top nav genuinely shrink for new users (surface streets can literally be narrower for people who don't need them) while everything stays reachable by search for the people who do. Also worth an actual desire-path study: watch *where people click when lost* (rage-clicks, search queries, support ticket phrasing) rather than only asking verbatim what feels bloated — the words people use to hunt for things will show you the real missing signposts, separate from the real missing features.

**4. Restaurant menu engineering — "on the menu" vs. "on the page"**
What I noticed: high-volume restaurants (Cheesecake Factory being the canonical example) carry huge menus because different regulars order completely different 3 dishes each, but they don't show a flat undifferentiated list — they use layout, sectioning, "chef's picks," and now QR/secondary menus to make a 200-item menu *read* as a short curated one on first glance.
Connection: this reframes "bloated" as a presentation/hierarchy failure rather than an inventory failure — you don't need fewer dishes on the menu, you need the first page to not try to show all of them with equal visual weight.
What to try: separate "exists and is reachable" from "is a default top-level item." Give each account (or each *role* within an account — admin vs. contributor) a small number of default-visible items chosen by their own usage pattern, with the rest one click away under something like "More" — not hidden, not removed, just not fighting for top-billing space for people who never touch them. This is the least structurally risky of the four since it changes prominence, not existence, of anything an enterprise account currently relies on.
