This is a logistics/operations problem, not primarily a design one — answering directly.

Here are a few outside-the-frame angles:

**1. Hospital pre-op scheduling (the "day-of surgery cancellation" problem)**
Hospitals discovered that most day-of surgery cancellations trace back to a handful of predictable causes — patient not cleared, equipment not confirmed, bed not ready — and built mandatory pre-op checklists with phone confirmation 24-48 hours out specifically because "the surgeon showed up and it didn't happen" is enormously expensive. Your three failure modes (customer absent, won't fit, elevator unreserved) are all *knowable in advance*, not random. Try: a mandatory pre-delivery call/text confirmation with a yes/no on presence, plus a measurement/elevator-reservation gate that must be checked off before a stop is even scheduled. This turns "failure discovered on arrival" into "failure discovered on the phone," which is far cheaper.

**2. Air traffic control's "ground delay" logic — push the wait upstream**
ATC doesn't let planes take off and then circle when a destination airport is jammed; it holds them on the ground where delay is cheap, not in the air where it's expensive. Your afternoon-window misses are the "circling" version of this — crews are absorbing schedule slip stop-by-stop instead of the slip being absorbed at dispatch. Try: build slack into the *morning* schedule (buffer time or fewer AM stops) rather than the afternoon, so drift doesn't compound into a 2pm cliff. Even a static rule — "AM route gets 20% slack, PM route gets none" — mirrors how ATC prices delay by where it's cheapest to absorb.

**3. Package-theft dashcam culture — evidence as a relationship tool, not a weapon**
The stairwell-damage dispute (3% of deliveries, "cannot prove either way") is structurally the same problem package thieves created for delivery companies, which got solved with cheap photo-on-completion proof, not surveillance. A phone photo of the walls before and after, timestamped, costs you nothing and doesn't require a new system — it just needs to become part of the crew's routine, the same way "photo of package at door" became routine once it started protecting drivers as much as customers.

**4. Airbnb's Superhost / co-host vetting funnel**
Your peak-weekend "can't find vetted second crew" problem is a marketplace trust problem, not a staffing problem — structurally close to how Airbnb solved "not enough good co-hosts" by building a light credentialing ladder (shadow a job, get rated by lead crews, unlock more shifts) rather than hiring-and-training in the traditional sense. Try: treat second-crew as a farm system — let your best regular crews nominate and vouch for people, with a one-shift paid trial rated by the lead — instead of a same-week vetted-hire scramble on peak weekends.

These are optional starting points, not a prescription — happy to go deeper on whichever one seems most workable given your systems and crew culture.
