Drawing on a saw-chain filer's craft — sharpening the cutter isn't the whole job; the depth gauge in front of each cutter has to be kept at the right *relative* height, filed down in step as the cutter wears back with every grind. My eye lives in the failure that doesn't look like a failure: a chain that's razor-sharp at the edge but starts throwing fine dust instead of chips because the gauge in front of it never got touched.

**1. The coupled-drift signature**
In my shop, a chain that "won't bite" is almost never a dull edge — it's two parts that were each fine on their own, but their *relative* offset drifted apart from actual use, silently, until the output pattern gives it away.

Your Saturdays throw the same signature: 8% waste *and* a specific shortage (croissants, two cafes) on the same day. That's not a volume problem where you're simply making too much or too little bread — it's the timing offset between cold-room hours for lamination and proofing-space hours for bread having drifted from what your head baker's mental schedule assumes.

Concrete move: for two Saturdays, actually log the clock times — when the cold room clears for lamination, when bread proofing needs to start — instead of going by what he assumes. If that gap has shrunk (say, lamination now runs later because croissant order volume has crept up over the months), you've found the drifted offset causing both the waste and the shortage at once, and it's a scheduling fix, not an oven-capacity or cold-room-size problem.

**2. One cutter out of angle throws the whole cut**
A chain only cuts straight if every cutter is filed to the same angle. One cutter off, even slightly, and the chain doesn't fail outright — it "leads," drifting off a straight line in a way that's hard to trace back to which tooth did it.

Cutting your head baker to four nights means someone else is filing two nights' worth of decisions. If their angle doesn't match his exactly, you won't get an obvious bad night — you'll get a slow systematic lead across the week that looks like noise.

Concrete move: before he cuts back, get his actual decision rule out of his head and onto paper as an explicit spec — specific quantities per cafe per weekday, and his substitution rule for what happens on a late text-in change. Then test it directly: have the second person run one night solo while he's off, and compare that night's output to his output on the same weekday, looking specifically for a consistent lead (over on one product, short on another) rather than just total waste.

**3. The gauge needs rechecking, not a one-time fix**
Rakers aren't something you set once. File them too far and the chain grabs and kicks; leave them untouched too long and it quietly stops biting. Either way, the fix is a small periodic recheck against a fixed number — not a single overhaul you file once and trust forever.

A written decision-rule spec for your night shift will have the same problem the day it's handed off. Cafe order patterns will shift, seasonal demand will move, and the spec will start silently drifting from reality exactly the way an unmaintained gauge does.

Concrete move: put a standing 15-minute monthly check on the calendar — actual Saturday waste % and shortage count against what the spec predicted — so drift shows up as a number crossing a threshold you set (say, waste back above 8% for two Saturdays running, or a repeat shortage at the same cafe), rather than as a complaint you hear about after the fact.
