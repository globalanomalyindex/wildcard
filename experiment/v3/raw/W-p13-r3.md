Drawing on telescope-making for this one, specifically the materials side of it: mirror substrates, coatings, and the difference between an objective and an eyepiece.

Three things surfaced that seem worth handing you.

**1. The aperture is not the eyepiece, and your roomier prototype confused them.**
A telescope's resolving power is set entirely by its objective, the mirror or lens that actually gathers light. The eyepiece never adds resolution; it only re-renders what the objective already captured, and a good optical shop keeps a set of parfocal eyepieces on one tube so swapping magnification never costs you your target, the image stays in registration. When your roomier layout hid columns, that wasn't a rendering change, it was a smaller objective: you cut the light being gathered, not just how it was displayed, which is exactly why veterans revolted. Concrete move: freeze one full data layer (all 18 columns, every load) as the thing that never shrinks, and build 2-3 swappable render modes on top of it, current dense table, a larger-type mode for aging-eye and new dispatchers, switchable per user without losing row position (that's the parfocal part). Test whether hostility disappears once nothing is actually removed, only re-rendered.

**2. Chromatic aberration gets fixed by adding an element, not by compromising the one you have.**
A single lens bends colors unevenly (that's chromatic aberration); the materials fix is an achromatic doublet, pairing a second glass with opposite dispersion so the two errors cancel, rather than grinding a compromise curve on one lens. Right now font size is carrying two jobs at once: numeral legibility for novices and exception-detection for veterans, and that's why every size you pick fails someone. Concrete move: add a redundant, non-numeral exception channel, a colored border, corner glyph, or icon badge on flagged rows, so exception-spotting doesn't require reading digits at all. Then re-test a larger base font size against veteran scan speed; if the badge is doing the real work, size should be free to move.

**3. Observatories keep a separate grab-and-go scope for public nights.**
Nobody hands a stranger the eyepiece of a research telescope tuned to 300x and calls it a demo, they use a distinct, forgiving wide-field instrument built for a first look. Concrete move: stop showing prospects the live 80-200 row console. Build a small curated demo view, a handful of illustrative loads, same visual language, purpose-built for cold viewers, and reserve the operational table for people who've actually put in the three months.
