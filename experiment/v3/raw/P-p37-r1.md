A few angles from outside the usual "buy a better reefer/scanner" frame:

**1. Treat the dock like an ER triage bay, not a loading zone.**
Hospitals solved a nearly identical problem: patients (product) arriving from multiple sources, needing rapid sort, with a clock that starts the moment they hit the door. Their fix wasn't better stretchers, it was a triage *protocol*: the first person who touches the patient does one thing only, tags severity, and routes — sorting and deep processing never happen in the same physical station. Right now your morning sort is probably doing "unload, identify, sort, stage" all at once, which is exactly why product idles above 40°F. Try physically splitting the dock into a 90-second "intake and tag" zone (cold-holding, not sorting) and a separate "sort and route" zone downstream, so nothing sits waiting for a human decision while exposed.

**2. Borrow the wine world's "provenance sticker," not a tech stack.**
You don't need a warehouse management system to solve traceability — high-end wine and cannabis both solved lot-tracing with something as dumb as a pre-printed sticker with a lot number and timestamp, slapped on at the boat/truck before anything else happens. The chef-rejection problem isn't really a software problem, it's a "the lot ID doesn't exist as a physical object on the box" problem. A $30 label printer at the point of unload (lot + supplier + landing time) would probably solve 80% of your traceability pain before you touch any inventory software.

**3. Reframe day-two discounted fish as a *pricing signal*, not a write-off.**
Airlines and event ticketing don't dump unsold inventory at a flat loss on a fixed day — they use dynamic markdown curves that start the moment risk becomes visible, not after it's realized. If you know by mid-morning what's trending toward day-two, you could push a small automatic discount to a *subset* of your 90 restaurants (the price-sensitive ones) hours earlier, rather than letting it ride to a full loss at the fish market. The lever isn't "sell it faster," it's "start the price moving before the deadline, not at it."

**4. Let the health inspector's requirement double as your management tool (compliance as instrumentation).**
Restaurant kitchens under health code now mostly use $15 wireless temp loggers (the kind meant for walk-in coolers) not because anyone loves paperwork, but because once temp is logged automatically, it becomes free data for everything else — you stop treating "prove compliance" and "find out where the dock is failing" as two separate problems. A couple of cheap Bluetooth loggers sitting *on the dock itself* (not just in the vans) would hand you both the inspector's paper trail and the exact window each morning when excursions happen — which is probably the actual root-cause diagnostic you need before redesigning the sort.
