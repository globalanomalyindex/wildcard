A few angles that sit outside "backup strategy" and "learn Node-RED better":

**1. Aerospace fail-safe design: separate the safety-critical path from the smart path.**
What actually enrages your partner isn't the Pi dying, it's that a *dumb wall switch* now depends on a *smart hub*. Commercial aircraft and elevators design around this: the fallback mode is never "the computer, but rebooted" — it's a simpler, independent mechanism that works even when the brain is gone. Applied here: rewire so the physical switch talks directly to the bulbs/relay (many Zigbee bulbs and smart switches support this, or a cheap relay in parallel) with automation as a layer on top, not the only path. That one change might buy you more domestic peace than any backup solution, and it's maybe one afternoon, not four hours a weekend for a month.

**2. DevOps "pets vs. cattle": stop nursing the SD card, make the Pi disposable.**
You're treating this Pi like a pet you keep resuscitating from memory. Infrastructure teams solved the equivalent problem by deciding individual servers shouldn't matter — corruption becomes "reflash and reprovision in 15 minutes," not "reconstruct from memory." Concretely: boot from a USB SSD instead of SD (this also kills the actual root cause — cheap SD cards die under Node-RED's constant writes), and keep the *provisioning steps* (not just a disk image) in a text file or small repo so a rebuild is mechanical, not a memory test.

**3. Legacy-code inheritance: you're not the original author anymore, you're maintaining someone else's system.**
Two years is long enough that "I wrote this" is no longer true in any useful sense — you're now a maintenance programmer inheriting a stranger's undocumented codebase who happens to share your name. The move software teams use isn't "understand it all again," it's incremental archaeology: export the flows as JSON, put them under version control, and only annotate a node the next time you touch it because something broke. You never do a big-bang re-learn; documentation accretes exactly where the fragility actually lives.

**4. Reframe the partner conflict as a reliability SLA, not a to-do list.**
"Fix the whole system" is a four-hour-a-weekend project that never finishes. "The wall switch always works, everything else is best-effort" is a one-sentence promise you can actually keep starting this weekend. Borrowing from how ops teams write service agreements: define the one thing that's not allowed to fail (bedroom lights respond to the switch) separately from everything that's allowed to degrade (automations, sensors, your Node-RED archaeology). That framing alone might do more for the relationship than the technical fix does.
