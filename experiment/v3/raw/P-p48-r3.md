Here are a few angles from outside the moving-checklist frame that might reshuffle how you're carrying this:

**1. The postal system as a leverage point, not a line item**
You're treating "forty accounts" as forty separate problems, but the USPS mail-forwarding system was basically built to solve exactly this. A single forwarding order (filed online, takes minutes) catches everything you haven't gotten to yet — bills, statements, the passport in transit — for up to a year, while you update accounts at your own pace instead of racing the move. This turns your one true fire drill (getting the passport safely) into a much smaller ask: confirm with the passport office whether they'll honor a forwarding order for a passport in transit, or request a hold/reroute directly (they often prefer the latter for secure mail). Either way, you're solving one high-precision problem instead of forty generic ones.

**2. Triage medicine's sorting logic**
ER triage doesn't rank by "how annoying" — it ranks by irreversibility and time-sensitivity. Worth sorting your list the same way: what happens if this misses its window and *can't* be undone (passport lost in the mail, movers rebooked at a new date) versus what's merely inconvenient if delayed (car registration, voter record, most of the 40 accounts, even the internet gap). Everything in the second bucket can sit in a holding pattern for weeks without real damage. That reframes "keep the logistics from multiplying" into "protect two or three irreversible items and let the rest be slow."

**3. Supply chain buffering for the internet gap**
A three-week install backlog against a mandatory ISP looks like a hard blocker, but supply chains treat this as a normal buffer problem, not a crisis — you don't rush the slow resource, you bridge it. A mobile hotspot (or tethering) during the gap decouples "I have internet" from "the ISP installed it," so the backlog stops being on your critical path at all. You just book the install date as early as possible and stop thinking about it until it happens.

**4. Treat the synchronous-hours problem as an async systems problem**
The "offices keep my hours" issue is the classic distributed-systems phone-tag problem, and the usual fix isn't finding more phone time — it's switching channels. Most of what you'd call about (electric transfer, DMV registration, address changes) has an online form or portal precisely because organizations know people can't call during business hours. Worth an hour up front auditing which of your forty items actually *require* a live call versus which have been quietly async-capable the whole time — it might shrink the "must call" list to just one or two genuinely stubborn ones (the ISP, maybe), which you then batch into a single PTO morning instead of scattering across five weeks.
