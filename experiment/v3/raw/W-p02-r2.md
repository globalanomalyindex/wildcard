Thinking about your migration through how river deltas handle forced change (and specifically the question every delta scientist has to ask: is a change in the record a real external signal, or an artifact of how the system organizes itself internally), a few things surfaced.

**1. Noticing.** A delta channel isn't upgraded in place. It aggrades, its gradient flattens, and past a threshold the river abandons it in one shift for a shorter, steeper path elsewhere, rather than being gradually widened where it sits.

**Mapping.** Your oldest cluster reads like a channel already past that threshold: two minor versions behind, ten weeks to end of support, running stateful services on local volumes pinned to specific nodes. An in-place rolling upgrade is asking that channel to keep carrying flow while you rebuild its bed underneath it.

**Concrete move.** For that cluster specifically, stand up a new node group (or new cluster) at the target version and cut traffic over in one scheduled window, instead of rotating the existing nodes under running pods. Treat the pinned local-PV data as its own separate operation inside that window: snapshot and restore into the new nodes, rather than trying to keep any one node alive through the version bump. The other two clusters aren't at that threshold yet, so a normal rolling upgrade may still suit them fine.

**2. Noticing.** When a delta's shape changes, the central question in my field is whether it's allogenic (a real external forcing: upstream sediment supply, sea level) or autogenic (internal channel dynamics that would happen regardless of any outside forcing). Mistaking one for the other means drawing the wrong conclusion about what's actually risky.

**Mapping.** Your one real data point on this migration, the 40-minute outage, came from a misconfigured admission webhook blocking scheduling cluster-wide. That's an autogenic failure, an artifact of execution, not a signal about the underlying compatibility of your 60 workloads. Worth checking whether that outage is doing more work in your risk estimate than the evidence supports.

**Concrete move.** Split the risk list into two buckets before the next attempt. Bucket one, the real signal: run a deprecated/removed-API scan across all manifests against the target version, confirmed hits go on the slow track with owner involvement. Bucket two, process risk: audit every admission webhook's `failurePolicy` and `namespaceSelector`, convert any cluster-wide fail-closed webhook to a scoped one, and prove its blast radius stays inside a single canary namespace before it's ever applied cluster-wide, since there's no staging cluster to test that any other way.

**3. Noticing.** A delta doesn't send water down every distributary channel equally. Flow partitions toward the path of least resistance, and the channel actually carrying sediment gets disproportionately more of the system's effort than the ones just passing clear water through.

**Mapping.** Your 8 teams don't have equal spare capacity this quarter, and on this reading they don't need equal effort either: only the workloads with confirmed deprecated-API usage (bucket one above) genuinely require owner hours.

**Concrete move.** Build one platform-run, zero-touch migration path (automated re-deploy or node-pool cutover) for every workload that comes back clean from the compatibility scan, so those teams do nothing. Reserve the scarce owner hours for the handful with confirmed deprecated-API usage, and bring each of those teams a specific, bounded ask ("fix these N call sites by date X") rather than a general request to help test the upgrade.
