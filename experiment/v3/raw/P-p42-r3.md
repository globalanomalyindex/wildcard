A few angles from outside the "backup strategy" box:

**1. Aircraft and elevators solve this with fail-safe defaults, not smarter brains.**
What I noticed: the actual emergency isn't "the Pi died," it's "the wall switch stopped working." That's a design failure, not a data-loss failure. Aircraft hydraulics and elevator control systems are built so that when the smart controller dies, the system falls back to a dumb, hardwired mode automatically, no software required. How it connects: your bedroom light switch is currently wired *through* your automation instead of *alongside* it. Try: check whether your Zigbee bulbs/switches support a local "always-last-state" or direct-pairing fallback so the physical switch keeps working even with the Pi unplugged. That one change alone might resolve the domestic conflict even if you never touch Node-RED again this month.

**2. Treat the Pi like livestock, not a pet, the way ops teams handle disposable servers.**
What I noticed: you're "rebuilding from memory" each time, which means the Pi itself is the source of truth, a single point of failure sitting on an SD card in a closet. Server operations teams gave up on lovingly-maintained individual machines years ago in favor of machines that are cheap to kill and trivial to respawn from a definition file. How it connects: your real asset isn't the SD card, it's the *flows and config*, which can live somewhere durable and versioned instead of only inside one card's flash memory. Try: next time you touch it, export the Node-RED flow JSON to a git repo (even just on your desktop, no GitHub account needed) as a five-minute add-on to whatever else you're doing. Now a corrupted card is a 20-minute reflash instead of a memory-jog.

**3. The "I no longer understand half the flows" feeling has a name in software archaeology: Chesterton's Fence.**
What I noticed: you're not just facing a hardware problem, you're facing an inherited-codebase problem, except you're the one who left it for yourself. The rule archaeologists and legacy-code engineers use is: before removing or "cleaning up" something you don't understand, figure out why it's there, because the mysterious flow is often load-bearing. How it connects: your four-hour weekend budget is exactly the kind of constraint that tempts a full rewrite, which is usually the wrong move on unclear legacy systems. Try: instead of rebuilding from memory, spend one weekend just annotating the existing flows with comment nodes ("this triggers X because Y") before changing anything. That converts "I don't understand this" into a permanent asset instead of a recurring tax you pay every failure.

**4. SD card corruption twice in one year is often an electrician's problem wearing a software costume.**
What I noticed: repeated SD corruption on a Pi is a well-known symptom of unclean power (a closet outlet, no surge protection, or the Pi being unplugged/power-cycled abruptly) rather than random bad luck. How it connects: this reframes the whole problem from "how do I recover faster" to "why does it keep breaking," which is a much cheaper fix. Try: a $10-15 UPS hat or even just a good-quality powered USB supply, plus switching the boot drive to boot from a small SSD/USB drive instead of SD (the Pi 4 supports this natively), which is dramatically more corruption-resistant. This might be the single highest-leverage move in your four hours, since it addresses root cause rather than the aftermath.
