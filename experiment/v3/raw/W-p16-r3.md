Drawing on camouflage, through a sensory-ecology lens: how concealment is only ever conditional. A pattern is never simply "hidden" or "bloated" in the abstract, it's hidden or exposed *from a specific detector, at a specific distance, under specific conditions.* That reframes your nav fight into three concrete moves.

**1. Two different detection channels, not one bloat problem**

Camouflage research treats pattern-matching and motion-detection as separate, largely independent visual pathways — an animal invisible on one can be caught instantly on the other, because motion detection doesn't care how good the pattern is.

Your two symptoms read the same way. "Can't find anything" is the pattern-matching channel: cold users scanning 13 unfamiliar items. The escalations after you moved one item are a different channel entirely: positional/motor memory in accounts that never scan, they just reach for a spot. A redesign aimed at the first channel does nothing for the second, and can actively trip it.

*Move:* split support tickets and verbatims into two tags — "can't-locate" (pattern channel) vs. "it-moved" (positional channel, probably underreported since it surfaces as an account-team escalation, not a ticket). Give them separate fixes: progressive disclosure/search for the first, and a stability guarantee for the second — a per-account nav order pinned at signup that never silently reflows, with any consolidation opt-in and brokered through the account team rather than pushed.

**2. "Hidden" is relative to the viewer, not a property of the layout**

A wing pattern that perfectly matches bark at a bird's scanning distance can look like an obviously wrong shape up close — both are true of the same wing at the same time, for different viewers. There's no single density that's correct for every viewing condition at once.

Your nav is being judged by two viewers under two totally different conditions on one surface: a new self-serve signup scanning 13 unfamiliar items cold, and a daily enterprise user who already knows exactly where their one item lives.

*Move:* give the two viewers two default views of the same underlying set, not two products. Ship a compressed first-run default for self-serve (5-6 items, chosen from what your usage data shows new accounts actually touch in week one, the rest under a labeled "more") while leaving existing/enterprise accounts' full 13-item view untouched by default. Measure week-4 activation and 90-day churn against that compressed default before touching the shared menu at all.

**3. "Still being used" is what mismatch looks like from the inside**

A hare that hasn't been eaten yet looks like proof its white coat still works, even while the snowline has been retreating for years. Local performance and population-level fitness diverge completely — "still working" isn't evidence against drift, it's exactly what drift looks like up close.

"Every nav item has a large account using it daily" is the same kind of evidence. It will be true forever, by construction, because it only tests whether anyone's still using an item, never whether the reason it was placed there still matches your current account mix. Usage data can't be the audit instrument here, it structurally never says remove anything.

*Move:* tag each of the 13 top-level items with the acquisition or deal that introduced it, and that cohort's share of *current* active accounts (not historical revenue). Pick a threshold, say founding-cohort share under 2% of active accounts, and demote items that cross it to a sub-nav or grouped area rather than delete, regardless of daily usage. That gives you a defensible, non-usage-based criterion for the one move usage data will never produce on its own.
