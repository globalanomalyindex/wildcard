Calligraphy turned out to be the draw, structure-and-form as the lens. Here's what actually held up against your problem, once I stopped treating it as a UI-density question and looked at it through what a scribe's page is actually doing.

**1. Trained fluency comes from repetition of the form, not from simplifying it.**
A scribe raised on a hand doesn't read a word letter by letter, they recognize the whole stroke-pattern (the ductus) at a glance, because they drilled that exact sequence hundreds of times in isolation before ever reading a full page at speed. Your veterans aren't reading the table's text either, they're pattern-matching row-shapes (position, color) the same way, and that fluency came from repetition, not from a friendlier layout. New dispatchers are still decoding character by character because nobody's training that pattern-recognition directly; they're absorbing it osmotically over three months of live traffic. Concrete move: build a standalone drill tool, entirely separate from the live screen, that isolates the 5 to 10 exception patterns veterans actually scan for and quizzes new hires on recognizing them fast and out of context, the way flashcards drill radicals before you ever read a sentence. Measure whether that cuts the three-month ramp, instead of trying to solve ramp time through layout at all.

**2. Hierarchy doesn't require enlarging the text; it can run on a second, low-resolution channel.**
A dense manuscript page stayed navigable not by making the body letters bigger but through rubrication: a thin band of red ink or a marginal mark layered on top, readable at a glance without parsing a single letter. Your roomier-layout prototype likely failed because it changed the layer veterans had trained on, the fine-grained text itself. Rubrication says don't touch that layer at all; add a second, independent channel that never competes with it. Concrete move: add a narrow strip per row, outside the existing 18 columns, not text, just 4 to 6 color or shape states for the exceptions that actually matter, so a new dispatcher can key off it peripherally in week one while the 11px table stays pixel-for-pixel what your veterans already read. A/B it against the current color-in-cells encoding for both novice ramp time and veteran scan speed; if it's really additive, veteran speed shouldn't move at all.

**3. Different readers got different hands from the same source text, not one hand stretched to fit everyone.**
A scribe didn't write one script for the altar Bible and the merchant's ledger. Textura for the page meant to be read slowly at a distance, chancery cursive for the page meant to be produced and read fast by a working professional, same content transcribed twice. Your prototype's mistake may not have been "roomier," it may have been offering it as an alternate mode of the veterans' own tool, asking one hand to serve two readers who need it to do opposite things. The sales-demo terror and the onboarding slog aren't UI problems on the dispatch screen at all, they're a wrong-hand problem. Concrete move: build the large-type, low-density version as a genuinely separate artifact (maybe not even branded as "the dispatch screen"), capped at 15 to 20 loads, its own column set, used only for sales demos and week-one onboarding, never offered to veterans as a toggle on their tool. That removes the political trigger (their tool being touched) entirely, and frees the roomy version's design from ever having to survive real 200-load monitoring.
