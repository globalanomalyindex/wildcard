Here are a few lenses from outside product/UX that might reframe this usefully. Take what's useful, ignore the rest.

**1. Political economy: concentrated benefits, diffuse costs**
Economist Mancur Olson studied why bad policies (subsidies, earmarks, tax loopholes) never get repealed even when almost everyone would be better off without them. The answer: the few who benefit a lot will organize and fight hard, while the many who lose a little each stay silent and disorganized. That's exactly your escalation pattern. Three enterprise accounts calling their reps is the organized minority defending a concrete win; the rising self-serve churn is the diffuse majority who never files a ticket, they just leave. This suggests the fix isn't "move the menu item," it's changing who bears the cost of change. Legislatures solve this with grandfather clauses and sunset provisions: new entrants get the new (lean) default, existing power users keep their configuration until it naturally lapses. Worth trying: ship the simplified nav only to new signups and accounts below some usage threshold, leave the 13-item nav untouched for the accounts who'd escalate. You're not removing anything from anyone who'd fight for it, you're just stopping the bloat from being everyone's default.

**2. Library and wayfinding science: comprehensiveness and findability are different problems**
A research library can hold four million volumes without feeling "bloated" to a visitor because the catalog and the collection are separate systems. Nobody browses the stacks to find a book, they use an index that's tuned for their query. Airports do the same thing: a hub with 150 gates doesn't shrink, it gives you a personal boarding-pass view of just your gate, your terminal. The lesson: your NPS verbatims about "bloated" may be a complaint about the *default view*, not the *inventory*. You may not have a "too many features" problem, you may have a "one shelf-arrangement forced on everyone" problem. Worth trying: treat the top nav as the "shelf" and build a query layer (smart search, "your workspace" personalization, role-based defaults) as the "catalog." Nothing has to move for the enterprise accounts who live in it daily; new users just never see the full shelf until they ask for it.

**3. Behavioral economics: you may be running two different studies on one storefront**
Sheena Iyengar's famous jam study found shoppers were more likely to buy from a 6-jam display than a 24-jam display, even though the large display drew more browsers. The number of options that helps *engagement* and the number that helps *conversion* aren't the same number. The Cheesecake Factory's notoriously huge menu works because regulars don't experience it as huge, they order what they always order, while the "bloated" perception hits first-time visitors hardest. Your two signals (NPS bloat complaints, and rising self-serve churn) and your other signal (daily active enterprise usage) might literally be two different populations rating two different experiences, not one population with mixed feelings. Worth trying: segment the "bloated" verbatims by account tenure and size before designing a fix. If the complaint clusters almost entirely in accounts under 90 days old, this stops being an information-architecture project and becomes an onboarding project, a much cheaper problem to solve.

**4. Ecology: manage by keystone-species triage, not uniform pruning**
In ecosystem management, you don't cut species by headcount, you map which ones are keystone (removing them collapses the system for many others) versus which are incidental to most of the environment but essential to one small niche. Your usage logs already gave you this map: every nav item has "at least one large account using it daily" isn't the same as every item being broadly load-bearing. Worth trying: score each of the 13 items and 9 settings tabs on breadth (how many accounts touch it) versus depth (how essential it is to the accounts that do). Broad-and-shallow items are prime candidates to fold into a "more" menu or search-only access; narrow-and-deep items are the ones you leave exactly where they are, or better, move behind a role/account-type toggle so only the dependent accounts see them at all.
