A few angles from outside the usual dashboard-design toolbox that might loosen this up:

**1. Bloomberg Terminal economics — density as a retention feature, not a debt to pay down.**
What I noticed: Bloomberg terminals are famously ugly, tiny-type, information-dense, and traders fight to keep them that way — the density itself is a moat, because years of muscle memory live in exact pixel positions. Newcomers hate it and take months too, and Bloomberg has made peace with that rather than "fixing" it. The connection: your veterans aren't defending the current table because it's good design, they're defending sunk-cost fluency. What you might try: instead of one layout that has to please everyone, build toward per-user saved column configs/views (dense "veteran" preset, roomier "onboarding" preset, curated "demo" preset) the same underlying data, different lenses — so you're not negotiating one screen's soul, you're letting different populations pick their own.

**2. Air traffic control's answer to "too many objects, too little screen."**
What I noticed: ATC solved a nearly identical problem decades ago — dozens to hundreds of tracked objects, experts who must catch exceptions instantly, and it did *not* solve it with smaller text. It solved it with preattentive symbolic coding: color, shape, and motion carry state so controllers detect anomalies by pop-out, not by reading. The connection: your 11px-and-18-columns approach assumes exception-spotting requires literal reading, but reading is slow and eye-straining precisely because it's not how trained perception actually works. What you might try: prototype a version where the 3-5 columns dispatchers scan for exceptions get a compact glyph/color signature (a "shape" per row state) instead of text, so pattern recognition — not reading — does the heavy lifting. That might be the lever that shrinks the 3-month ramp without shrinking the columns.

**3. Chess chunking research — the 3-month ramp might not be a UI problem at all.**
What I noticed: Chase & Simon's classic chess studies found grandmasters don't "read" a board faster, they perceive it in learned chunks — meaningless to a novice, instantly legible to an expert, and that chunking takes exactly this kind of months-long exposure to build, regardless of board design. The connection: if the 3-month ramp is chunk-formation time rather than a legibility problem, no amount of resizing will shortcut it, and your prototype "revolt" is evidence for this, not against your prototype. What you might try: stop trying to make the dense table faster to learn, and instead build a deliberate chunking scaffold for new hires — a training mode that overlays plain-language labels on the exact same dense layout new hires will graduate into, so ramp-up is explicit skill-building rather than an accidental tax.

**4. The restaurant with two menus — reframing the sales-demo failure.**
What I noticed: a diner regular wants the wall of 200 items because they already know what they want and scanning it fast is the whole point; a first-time tourist wants a curated 12-item menu, and no restaurant thinks it's dishonest to print both. The connection: your sales team is trying to close small fleets using the same artifact you built for 200-load power users — that's not a dispatch-screen flaw, it's showing someone shopping for a sedan the cockpit of a race car. What you might try: treat the sales-demo view as an intentionally different, honest product surface (a "starter" dispatcher view scaled to 10-20 loads) rather than a compromise on the real screen — this also gives you a legitimate on-ramp product for small fleets instead of a screen they're afraid of.
