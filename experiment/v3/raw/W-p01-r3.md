A bit of rotational dynamics kept surfacing while I sat with your problem, and three threads from it actually earned their place.

**1. The force needed to hold a curve scales with speed squared over radius, and the cheap fix is usually the radius, not the force.**
In circular motion, F = mv²/r: doubling speed quadruples the force you need to hold the same radius, but doubling the radius only halves it. The cheap lever is almost always the radius. Your "speed" is the events table's growth; your "radius" is how much of that table every 90-day query has to sweep, which today is effectively the whole thing. Native range-partitioning the events table by time (say, monthly) bounds that radius to a constant handful of partitions per query, roughly 3-4 for a 90-day window, no matter how large the historical archive behind it grows. Partition pruning happens at the planner level as long as the partition key shows up in the WHERE clause your ORM already emits, so you don't need to touch ORM-generated SQL. The one irreducible piece, building the partitioned table alongside the live one and doing the final cutover, is exactly the kind of work that fits a Sunday-night window.

**2. That force has to be supplied fresh, continuously; starve it and the system doesn't blow up, it just quietly reverts to its default shape.**
Cut off centripetal force and an object doesn't fly outward, it simply goes straight, the shape it wanted all along. Autovacuum is playing that role for your table's visibility map. When it falls behind during business hours, nothing errors, the table just drifts toward its default state: stale visibility bits force index-only scans to fall back to fetching from the heap row by row, silently. That's a very plausible chunk of your 200ms → 8s creep. Worth checking directly: run EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) on one of the 90-day aggregates and look at "Heap Fetches." A large, growing count confirms it. You're on Postgres 13, which added insert-triggered autovacuum (autovacuum_vacuum_insert_scale_factor / _threshold) specifically for append-heavy tables like this one, separate from the old dead-tuple-only trigger. A per-table override, e.g. ALTER TABLE events SET (autovacuum_vacuum_insert_scale_factor = 0.02), aims that force where the churn actually is instead of relying on a global default sized for a mixed workload.

**3. What counts as "the quiet center" is relative to a reference frame, and frames drift.**
An orbit that looks calm from one center looks like it's flying apart the moment the center itself moves. Your nightly reporting job was almost certainly scheduled around an implicit single quiet window, probably wherever your team or provider's defaults sat, that made sense when your customers were less spread out. As usage globalized, Europe's morning peak drifted into what used to be the quiet zone, and the job's fixed schedule didn't move with it. Chasing a new quiet hour just sets up the same collision again once you add the next region. The sturdier move is to stop needing a center at all: replace the one heavy nightly batch with an incrementally refreshed materialized view or rollup table (native REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY, or a small hourly job that appends the latest delta), so there's no single scheduled spike whose timing has to keep chasing your traffic's geography.
