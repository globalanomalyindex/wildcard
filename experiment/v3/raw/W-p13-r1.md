I cut cylinder seals in chalcedony, narrative friezes rolled into wet clay. My whole practice lives on measurement: how much story fits in a strip a few centimeters tall before the circumference runs out, and where the roll has to close without a seam.

**1. The resolution floor**
Chalcedony under the cutting wheel has a hard resolution floor: past a certain point the tool can't render finer detail, no matter how badly a figure needs it. So I never spend that floor evenly. A god gets full modeling; the filler between figures (rosettes, drill-point stars) goes cheap and schematic on purpose, because giving everything equal fidelity is what actually makes a packed frieze unreadable, not the density itself.

Your 18 columns are all rendered at the same 11px fidelity, which is the equal-weight version of that mistake. Rank the columns by how often veterans actually use them for exception-spotting, give the top 4 to 6 full-fidelity text at 13-14px, and convert the remaining 12 to 14 into compact glyph cells (color chips, single-letter codes, sparkline-style marks). Nothing gets hidden or scrolled; the width you free by compressing the low-priority columns is what buys the legibility bump on the columns that matter most.

**2. The presentation roll**
The same cylinder produces two different impressions depending on how it's rolled: one full, unbroken rotation for a formal piece, and quick, overlapping partial rolls for routine sealing, where the tablet only needs enough of the mark to be valid. It's the same carved object serving two jobs by how it's applied, not by being recut.

Stop asking one dense table to be both the dispatcher's daily instrument and sales' first impression. Build a separate demo preset off the same data: 15-20 curated loads, the 5-7 columns that tell the clearest story, larger type, no scroll, used only in sales demos. The operational table stays exactly as dense as your veterans need it.

**3. The owner's inscription**
Even a seal packed with figures a stranger couldn't parse usually carries one small fixed inscription, the owner's name and title, cut in plain, simple signs, so anyone receiving the tablet can read at least that much cold. One guaranteed-legible anchor lets an untrained viewer get something true out of a scene they otherwise can't decode.

Give every row one plain-language anchor column: a single word (ON TIME, LATE, AT RISK) in full-size, uncoded text, positioned identically on every row. A brand-new dispatcher or a prospect in a demo can read that one column and get real signal immediately, while the other 17 stay exactly as coded and dense as your veterans have trained on.
