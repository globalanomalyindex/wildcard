Here are a few outside-the-frame angles that might be useful alongside the standard "upgrade runbook" thinking.

**1. Trauma surgery's "damage control" doctrine**
What I noticed: you're describing a patient with multiple injuries and a hard clock, and the instinct is to fix everything in one long operation. Trauma surgeons deliberately don't do that — when a patient is unstable, they do the minimum needed to stop the bleeding (pack the wound, stop hemorrhage, close up), then send the patient to recover before a second, planned surgery does the definitive repair. Operating too long on an unstable patient kills them regardless of how good the individual repairs are.
Connection: your ten-week deadline is really only about *not lapsing into unsupported*, not about fully modernizing every workload. Those are two different operations.
Worth trying: split explicitly into "stop the bleeding" (get the oldest cluster's control plane current enough to stay supported, even if some node groups and stateful services stay untouched) versus "definitive repair" (deprecated API fixes, PV migration off node-pinning) as a second, unhurried phase after the deadline pressure is gone.

**2. Epidemiology's ring vaccination / containment ordering**
What I noticed: your riskiest elements (local-PV pinned statefuls, deprecated-API workloads) are being treated as things to get through, when they're actually the thing that should determine sequencing.
Connection: ring vaccination doesn't inoculate randomly or by convenience — it maps out the highest-transmission nodes first and works outward, precisely because untouched high-risk nodes are where outbreaks restart. Your equivalent: map which workloads/teams are "patient zero" (deprecated API + local PV + low team capacity, all three at once) and treat that short list as its own tier with dedicated attention, rather than folding it into a uniform 60-workload rollout.

**3. Electrical engineering's fail-open vs. fail-closed convention**
What I noticed: the 40-minute outage happened because an admission webhook, when it broke, defaulted to *blocking* all scheduling — a fail-closed failure mode applied globally.
Connection: fuses and circuit breakers are designed so that when the protective mechanism itself fails, it fails toward safety, not toward total shutdown. Applying that lens to admission control: your webhook config is a single point that can take down scheduling cluster-wide by design, independent of what triggers it.
Worth trying: before touching anything else, audit `failurePolicy` on admission webhooks and set critical ones to `Ignore` (fail-open) or scope them with `namespaceSelector`/`objectSelector` so a bad webhook can only block a namespace, not the cluster. That's a cheap, high-leverage fix that removes the scariest variable from the whole upgrade.

**4. Negotiation's "minimum viable ask"**
What I noticed: you're treating this as needing buy-in from eight teams who have little slack, which reads like a big organizational lift.
Connection: negotiators dealing with parties who have limited bandwidth don't ask for broad cooperation — they identify the smallest specific commitment that unblocks them, per party. Most of your eight teams probably don't need to do anything (their workloads aren't touching deprecated APIs or local PVs); only a handful do.
Worth trying: instead of a general "we're upgrading, please prepare" message to all eight, send each team a one-line, workload-specific ask ("this deployment references `extensions/v1beta1 Ingress`, needs a manifest change by [date]") — and say nothing to the teams with nothing to do. Shrinking the ask to exactly what's load-bearing usually gets faster action than a shared deadline.
