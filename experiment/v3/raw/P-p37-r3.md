Here are a few angles from outside the cold-chain/logistics playbook that might be worth stealing from:

**1. Blood bank chain-of-custody, not warehouse inventory**
What I noticed: your traceability gap and your temperature gap are actually the same gap, just showing up in two places. Blood banks solved exactly this because a bag out of range for even minutes has to be pulled and traced instantly, not investigated later. Their trick isn't a big system, it's that every unit gets a single barcode at intake that carries lot, source, and time, and it gets scanned at every handoff, not just logged on a clipboard once. Might be worth trying: put a barcode or QR on each case at the supplier (not at your dock), scan it the moment it lands and again when it leaves the dock. That one scan-in event does double duty, it timestamps the excursion risk and it kills the afternoon of paper-digging when a chef rejects a box.

**2. Pharma cold-chain loggers instead of a monitoring process**
What I noticed: you're trying to solve "we don't have temperature records" as a process problem (someone should log it) when vaccine and pharma shippers solved it as a hardware problem. A $10-30 disposable Bluetooth or USB temp logger travels inside the case itself and produces a printable excursion report on demand. Might be worth trying: put one logger per pallet or per supplier lot rather than trying to police the dock behavior. When the inspector asks, you hand them a report instead of reconstructing your morning from memory.

**3. ER mass-casualty triage tags for the sorting bottleneck**
What I noticed: the dock sort is your actual failure point, not the road, and it sounds like a flood-of-simultaneous-inputs problem, which is exactly what ER triage tagging was built for. They don't chart everyone on arrival, they snap a colored tag on each patient in seconds that encodes urgency, and sorting happens by tag color, not by working a list top to bottom. Might be worth trying: color-tag cases by how perishable or slow-moving that species/lot is right as it comes off the truck, so the sort prioritizes by what will spoil first rather than by whatever order it happens to hit the dock. Less time in the danger zone for the stuff that can least afford it.

**4. Airline day-of-departure pricing instead of a day-two dump**
What I noticed: your day-two discount to the fish market is a fixed, late, all-or-nothing move, which is the same shape as an airline selling an empty seat. Airlines don't wait until the gate closes to discount, they release inventory in shrinking windows as the deadline nears, often to a standing list of buyers. Might be worth trying: instead of one cliff at day two, send a same-day flash alert late on day one, before spoilage risk is fully baked in, to a rotating list of price-sensitive restaurants or a market contact. You likely recover more than the eventual dump price, and you get an earlier signal on what's overbought.

All four point the same direction, really: push tagging/sensing to the point of origin (supplier or truck) rather than trying to catch problems at your dock after the fact. Curious if any of these map onto constraints you already know rule them out.
