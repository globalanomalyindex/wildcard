Here are a few angles from outside the usual "bakery ops" frame — treat these as things to poke at, not a prescription.

**1. The newsvendor problem (classic operations research)**
What you're describing — simultaneous waste *and* shortage on the same day — is the textbook signature of a mismatched service level, not bad luck. Newspaper sellers, fashion retailers, and blood banks have all solved variants of "how much perishable stock to make when demand is uncertain and leftovers are worthless." The fix isn't better guessing, it's setting a deliberate stocking ratio per SKU per cafe (based on the cost of a wasted croissant vs. the cost of an angry cafe), rather than one house intuition applied everywhere. Worth trying: pull your last 8-12 Saturdays and see if the *same* two cafes are chronically shorted — that's a signal your baker's mental model is calibrated for the average cafe, not the outliers.

**2. Air traffic control's "manifest lock"**
Text and voicemail orders trickling in until 10pm, followed by an 11pm mix start, is a system with no controlled airspace — everything's first-come-first-served chaos right up to the wire. ATC (and airline gate manifests, and commercial kitchens' ticket rails) solve this with a hard, visible cutoff: after time X, the manifest is frozen and *no* amount of urgency reopens it. The interesting move isn't just "set a cutoff" (you probably already want that) — it's making the cutoff a *cafe-facing feature*, not a bakery-facing constraint: "orders locked at 8pm, here's your standing order unless changed" turns a source of friction into a reliability promise you can market.

**3. Hospital bed-board scheduling (shared recovery space)**
Two ovens producing goods that then compete for the *same* recovery space (proofing rack / cold room) at the *same* hours is structurally identical to a hospital where two surgical services share one recovery ward — cardiac patients and ortho patients both need beds at 2pm. Hospitals solve this with block scheduling: each service gets a *reserved window*, not first-dibs chaos. Worth trying: instead of bread and laminated dough negotiating space in real time, give each a fixed, non-overlapping window even if it means shifting when laminated dough gets made in the day — sometimes the fix is moving the process, not expanding the room.

**4. Tacit knowledge as a single point of failure**
A head baker who "keeps the schedule in his head" is the bakery's version of the one engineer who's the only one who knows how the production system actually deploys — every ops team has a name for this (bus factor, key-person risk). The fact that he wants to *cut back* nights is the forcing function, not a scheduling inconvenience — it's an unplanned knowledge-transfer deadline. Rather than treating "get him to 4 nights" as a staffing problem, it might be worth treating it as a debrief project: have someone shadow him for two weeks specifically to externalize the heuristics he's applying (why does croissant dough get priority over bread proofing on Fridays, e.g.) before the fourth night disappears.
