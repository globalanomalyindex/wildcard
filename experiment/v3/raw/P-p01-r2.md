Here are a few angles that sit outside the standard "add an index, tune autovacuum" playbook. Take or leave any of them.

**1. Log-structured storage / LSM-tree compaction**
What I noticed: your events table isn't really behaving like a relational table anymore, it's behaving like a write-heavy log, which is exactly the shape that Cassandra, RocksDB, and time-series DBs (InfluxDB, TimescaleDB) were built around. Their answer isn't "vacuum harder," it's tiering: hot recent data stays in a fast mutable form, older data gets compacted into a read-optimized, mostly-immutable form on its own schedule.
How it connects: your 90-day queries are scanning a mix of "still being written to" rows and "settled, will never change again" rows through the same machinery. That's the mismatch.
What to try: partition by time (native declarative partitioning, which Postgres 13 supports) so that only the last day or two of partitions are ever touched by autovacuum's expensive path, and older partitions can be marked read-only / vacuum-freeze'd once and left alone. This is a Sunday-night-safe change since you can build new partitions and migrate incrementally.

**2. Generational garbage collection**
What I noticed: "autovacuum falls behind during business hours" is structurally the same complaint as "our GC can't keep up and causes stop-the-world pauses." Runtimes solved this decades ago with generational collectors: short-lived objects get their own small, cheap, frequently-swept space; long-lived objects move to an older generation that's swept rarely.
How it connects: right now every row in your events table, whether it's a minute old or a year old, is competing for the same vacuum worker's attention.
What to try: the same time-based partitioning above gives you this almost for free, the "young generation" (today's partition) can have aggressive per-partition autovacuum settings (lower scale factor, more workers) since it's small, while "old generation" partitions need essentially none.

**3. Accounting's "closing the books"**
What I noticed: finance teams face the same problem you do, aggregating over a big rolling window that includes both settled and still-moving numbers, and they solved it hundreds of years before computers by never recomputing the past. Closed periods get a permanent snapshot; only the current open period gets recalculated.
How it connects: your 90-day rollups are recomputing day 1 through day 89 every single time, even though those days are done and will never change.
What to try: a materialized rollup table (daily pre-aggregates for anything older than, say, yesterday) that your ORM's queries could join against instead of the raw table for the "closed" portion. If you can't touch ORM query text, you might be able to get most of the win with a view the ORM already selects from, swapped underneath it.

**4. "Follow the sun" ops scheduling**
What I noticed: the nightly-batch-collides-with-EU-morning problem is a timezone problem dressed up as a database problem, it's the same failure mode multinational support teams hit when they schedule "the big handoff" at one fixed wall-clock time instead of tracking where the trough actually is.
How it connects: a single nightly window assumes there's a quiet global moment, there isn't one anymore if you have EU users.
What to try: instead of one large batch job, spread the reporting work into smaller continuous increments that each region's trough can absorb, so no single run needs a big contiguous quiet window at all. This pairs well with #3, since incremental rollups are naturally the kind of small, frequent work that suits this.
