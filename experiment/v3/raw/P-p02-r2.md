Here are a few outside-the-box angles that might reframe parts of this — take or leave any of them.

**1. Options theory: the "ten weeks" may be a price, not a wall.**
You're treating end-of-support as a fixed deadline, but AWS sells EKS Extended Support for exactly this situation — a paid year of continued patches on the old version. Financially this is just buying a call option: pay a premium to decouple "must upgrade" from "safe to upgrade." Given that the last attempt caused a 40-minute outage and you have no representative staging environment, it might be cheaper to buy a few extra months of runway than to compress a genuinely risky migration into ten weeks with eight teams who have no spare capacity. Worth pricing out before treating the deadline as non-negotiable.

**2. Aviation: Minimum Equipment List + pre-flight checklist.**
Airlines don't ground a plane for every broken non-critical system — they fly with a formally logged, time-boxed exception (MEL item) and a compensating procedure, reviewed on a fixed schedule. Your deprecated-API workloads could get the same treatment: an explicit, dated waiver list per team instead of an implicit "we'll get to it," which turns a vague risk into a tracked one with an expiration date. Separately, that admission-webhook outage is exactly the class of error a pre-flight checklist exists to catch — a short, mandatory, boring checklist run before every cluster change (not a runbook nobody reads) is how aviation and surgery independently converged on preventing this exact failure mode.

**3. Structural engineering: build the bypass before you touch the load-bearing bridge.**
When engineers replace a bridge that can't be closed, they often build a temporary parallel structure and shift traffic onto it, rather than swap components under live load. You have no staging cluster — which is normally the argument for testing changes safely, but it's also an argument for treating "spin up a new cluster on the target version alongside the old one" as the default move, especially for the stateful, node-pinned services where in-place node replacement is the riskiest part of your plan. It costs more compute temporarily, but it converts "risky live surgery" into "build new, cut over, decommission old."

**4. Epidemiology + wildfire response: ring containment and unified command.**
Ring vaccination contains an outbreak by immunizing a small radius around a case before it spreads — the analog here is upgrading in deliberately small, blast-radius-limited rings (one low-stakes namespace or team first, expanding outward) so a bad admission webhook only ever hits a ring, not the cluster. And for the coordination problem — eight teams, low spare capacity, one shared deadline — wildfire Incident Command System exists precisely because scarce responders can't self-organize under time pressure: one incident commander, explicit resource requests, teams pull tasks rather than getting assigned ambiguous ownership. Might be worth borrowing the role structure even if nothing else.
