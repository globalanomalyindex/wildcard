I drew a coral micro-fragmenting technician — reef restoration nursery work — with the lens turned to time-and-rhythm.

I cut slow-growing massive corals into dime-sized chips for a living, because a coral that's just been cut drops everything to heal the wound, and grows tens of times faster doing that than an intact colony ever grows on its own. What I obsess over is that none of my clocks agree — healing time, tide time, fouling time all run at different rates — and the nursery only works if I stop pretending they're one calendar.

A few things surfaced when I looked at your setup:

**1. A whole colony almost never crosses its own growth threshold; fifty coin-sized fragments each cross theirs in days.**
Cutting the coral doesn't just make it smaller — it changes which threshold fires, and how fast. Right now your events table is one colony: autovacuum's dead-tuple trigger (20% of the table, by default) has to accumulate across all ~600GB before it fires, and once it fires the cleanup job itself is sized to match — a slow-moving operation contending with live traffic all day. Partition the table by time (weekly or monthly ranges — `pg_partman` will manage this on PG13), and that threshold gets evaluated per-partition instead: each hot partition crosses it at a small absolute row count and gets vacuumed in place, fast, while last month's partitions barely accumulate dead tuples at all. Side benefit that matters given your ORM constraint: partition pruning is automatic — a generated `WHERE created_at > now() - interval '90 days'` clause gets the planner to skip everything outside that range without you touching a query. And since you only get Sunday nights, do the cut in tranches: create the partitioned table alongside the original, dual-write new rows into both, then migrate historical data one partition (oldest first) per Sunday window rather than one 600GB rewrite.

**2. Tide windows for working the nursery aren't fixed — they slide about 50 minutes later every day and swing through a two-week spring/neap cycle, so a maintenance slot I picked by calendar last year has usually drifted out from under me by now.**
The failure isn't picking a bad window — it's trusting a fixed clock time instead of re-checking where the real quiet period sits today. Pull hourly load and latency telemetry split by customer region for the last few weeks, find where the actual trough is now (not a year ago, and not assuming it's still nighttime-anchored given EU growth), and move the reporting job there. Then put a recurring quarterly check on the calendar — the trough will keep moving as the EU book of business grows, the same way the tide keeps moving regardless of what the schedule says.

**3. A fragment that's done healing gets outplanted onto the reef promptly — left on the nursery tree, it keeps costing me light and flow that the next cohort needs.**
Data that's aged out of active use still costs you if it stays in the working set. Once a partition ages past your 90-day aggregation window, detach it and move it off the hot tablespace (or just detach it — `pg_partman`'s retention policy can do this on a schedule). It shrinks what your buffer cache and autovacuum are actively carrying, on top of whatever the partitioning itself already bought you.
