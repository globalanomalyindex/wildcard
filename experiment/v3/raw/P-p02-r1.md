Here are a few outside-the-frame angles on this — take what's useful, leave the rest.

**1. Hospital ward decanting (phased occupied renovation)**
What I noticed: hospitals renovate wards that can never fully close by "decanting" — moving patients out unit by unit into swing space, renovating the empty unit, then reversing the flow. Nobody moves the whole hospital at once, and no unit is ever both occupied and under construction.
How it connects: your local-PV pinned stateful services are the patients who can't just be wheeled anywhere — they need a compatible bed (node) prepared first. Your real constraint isn't cluster version, it's "where does workload X live while its node gets replaced."
What you might try: build one small "swing" node group on the new version first, migrate the least-risky stateful service into it as a dry run, then use that same swing group as the landing zone for the next one, effectively rolling capacity through the fleet instead of upgrading in place. It also gives you a rollback bed if a migration goes wrong.

**2. Aviation's Minimum Equipment List and forcing functions**
What I noticed: airlines don't ask "is everything perfect" before every flight — they maintain an MEL, a pre-agreed list of what's allowed to be broken and what absolutely grounds the plane. Your webhook outage was a single unlisted item taking down the whole fleet because nothing gated it.
How it connects: the 40-minute outage wasn't really an upgrade problem, it was a missing circuit-breaker problem — one webhook had blast radius over all pod scheduling. That's a systemic gap independent of version skew, and it's cheap to fix now versus during crunch week.
What you might try: before touching versions, add failure-mode isolation — failurePolicy: Ignore (or scoped namespaceSelectors) on every admission webhook, so a bad webhook degrades gracefully instead of halting scheduling cluster-wide. That one change de-risks every future upgrade, not just this one.

**3. Debt refinancing — buy time as its own line item**
What I noticed: when a company can't pay off debt by the maturity date, the immediate move often isn't "faster repayment," it's negotiating an extension or bridge loan to convert a hard deadline into a soft one, buying room to execute the real plan calmly.
How it connects: AWS sells exactly this instrument — Extended Support for EKS control planes past standard end-of-support, at a per-cluster-hour premium. Ten weeks with 8 teams at low capacity is a classic "we need bridge financing" situation, not a "sprint harder" situation.
What you might try: price out Extended Support for the oldest cluster as your explicit fallback/insurance, and communicate it upward now as "the deadline is soft if we choose to pay for it" — this reframes the project from a cliff-edge deadline to a cost-vs-speed tradeoff you control, which usually changes how much cooperation you get from the eight teams.

**4. Controlled burn / firebreak before the real fire**
What I noticed: forestry crews don't fight a wildfire by improvising containment lines in the middle of it — they pre-cut firebreaks and run small controlled burns in calm conditions specifically to learn how the fuel behaves before the uncontrolled version arrives.
How it connects: you have no staging cluster that mirrors production, which means your first real signal about deprecated-API breakage or webhook behavior under the new version is production itself — exactly what happened last time.
What you might try: rather than building a full staging mirror (expensive, ten weeks is tight), carve a small low-traffic node group inside one existing cluster, upgrade only that group, and cut over 2-3 of the least critical of the ~60 workloads to run live traffic there for a week. It's a controlled burn at survivable scale that surfaces admission-webhook and API-deprecation surprises before they touch the other 55+ workloads.
