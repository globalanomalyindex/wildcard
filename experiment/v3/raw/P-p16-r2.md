Here are a few angles that come from outside product-IA thinking but map cleanly onto your numbers:

**1. Airport terminal signage, not highway signage**
What I noticed: airports solved a similar problem decades ago — hundreds of gates, shops, and services, way more "nav items" than any single traveler needs — by making the sign personal (your boarding pass gates the info) rather than shrinking the airport. Nobody complains United's terminal is "bloated" because they only ever see their gate.
How it connects: your "every item has a heavy user" data isn't an argument for a universal 13-item bar, it's an argument that no two accounts should be looking at the same bar. The nav is bloated as a *shared* object, not necessarily for any given user.
What to try: instead of debating which items to cut, test collapsing the nav to a role/usage-derived default (recently-used + starred) with everything else one search or "more" click away — full inventory preserved, display personalized. This sidesteps the "you moved my menu item" escalation because nothing is removed, only reordered per-account.

**2. Restaurant menu engineering**
What I noticed: the Cheesecake Factory has a 21-page menu and doesn't get "bloated" complaints nearly as often as diners get lost at restaurants with a third as many items — the difference is categorization and visual hierarchy, not item count. Menu engineering also classifies items on a popularity-vs-profitability grid (stars, plow-horses, puzzles, dogs) rather than by raw usage.
How it connects: you're treating "13 items" as the disease when the verbatims say "can't find anything" — that's a findability complaint, which can be fixed without touching count. And "usage" alone (your current filter) is the wrong axis — you need usage *breadth* (how many accounts, not just whether one whale uses it daily) crossed with usage depth.
What to try: build the two-axis grid — % of accounts touching each nav item (breadth) × frequency among touchers (depth). Items that are high-depth/low-breadth (one enterprise account's daily habit) are exactly the ones to demote into a personalized/pinned space rather than global top nav — protecting the whale without taxing the other 59,999 teams' scan time.

**3. A statistics gut-check on your own evidence**
What I noticed: with 13 nav items and presumably hundreds of large accounts, it would be statistically odd if some large account *didn't* touch each item daily — that's closer to a pigeonhole guarantee than a signal of broad value. This is the same shape as survivorship-bias arguments (Wald's bullet holes).
How it connects: "every item has heavy usage" currently functions as a veto on any change, but it's true almost by construction and says nothing about whether the item deserves permanent top-level, un-collapsible placement for everyone.
What to try: reframe the metric from "does anyone use this daily" to "what's the cost of one extra click for this item's non-power-users vs. the cost of a scan-tax on the 59,000 teams who never touch it." That's a defensible, quantifiable tradeoff instead of an unfalsifiable usage floor.

**4. How OSes deprecate things without riots**
What I noticed: Apple/Microsoft move or remove UI constantly, but avoid revolt by never doing it as a single flip for everyone — new users get the new default, existing power users get a long opt-out/legacy window, sometimes permanently for enterprise. The pain you hit (three enterprise escalations in a week) was a *rollout* failure as much as a design one — everyone got the change simultaneously with no consent.
How it connects: your two symptoms (support tickets from confusion, enterprise escalation from change) may need two different mechanisms, not one redesign — new self-serve signups can get a clean default nav from day one (no one escalates a nav they never had), while existing accounts stay pinned to their current layout until they self-opt-in.
What to try: ship the simplified/personalized nav as the *default for new signups only* first. That directly targets your creeping self-serve churn without touching a single enterprise account's muscle memory — turning the "we can't change it without an uproar" constraint into "we don't have to change it for them at all."
