Drawing on a water gilder — someone who builds picture frames the old way: rabbit-skin size, whiting gesso, clay bole, gold leaf, agate burnisher. Their whole craft runs on things flowing on a clock they don't control — heat leaking out of a glue pot, moisture wicking a few microns into a clay ground, a coat crossing from "too wet to work" to "too dry to work" in a window measured in minutes. Here's what surfaced looking at your bakery through that lens.

**1. Route his presence, don't just document his hours**

In my shop, technique doesn't live only in the master gilder's hands — it lives in a written ground recipe (glue-to-chalk ratios, number of coats, dry time between each) precisely so an apprentice can run the routine coats alone, and the master only has to show up for the moves that actually need judgment.

Your head baker's schedule is the same kind of asset stuck in the same kind of bottleneck: it's all in his head, so every night costs him equally, whether that night is routine or genuinely volatile. Write the routine part down as an actual playbook — standard par counts by cafe and day-of-week, station assignments, what runs on autopilot — and use it to free him from the predictable nights entirely. Then his four nights aren't "four instead of six," they're the two or three nights that actually carry irreducible judgment (Friday into Saturday, most likely, given where your waste already concentrates) plus whichever routine nights he wants to keep an eye on. That's a different negotiation than "can we survive on fewer nights" — it's "which nights need him."

**2. Stop scheduling the room-conflict, zone it instead**

Gilding studios don't time-slice one bench between wet work and dry work — a humid gilding station and a dry burnishing station contaminate each other if you make them share space on a rota, so the real fix was never a better schedule, it was a partition: two zones with different air, coexisting in the same footprint.

Your cold room and your proofing space are the same trap: lamination wants cold and dry, proofed bread wants warm and humid, and no rota reconciles two opposite environments fighting for the same hours. Before you spend more effort scheduling around that conflict, price out decoupling it physically — even a curtained-off cold cabinet or a small dedicated proofer cart pulled out of the shared room. If it pencils out, the conflict stops needing a schedule at all.

**3. Cap the rewrites, not just the clock**

Rabbit-skin glue is thermoreversible — you can reheat it if it gels on you, but only so many times before the collagen itself weakens and the bond gets worse than if you'd just mixed a fresh batch. The damage isn't from working late; it's from how many times the same batch gets re-melted.

Your 10pm cutoff is aimed at the wrong variable for the same reason. It's not really *when* the last change lands that produces the 8% Saturday waste and the shorted croissants — it's how many times the mental plan gets silently re-melted between the first voicemail and 11pm mixing. Try locking a draft plan at a fixed earlier point (9pm, say) and capping it at two amendments before mix — after that, a late order is a tomorrow problem, not a re-cut-everything problem. That forces the real fight to happen at 9, where there's still slack to absorb it, instead of at 11, where there isn't.
