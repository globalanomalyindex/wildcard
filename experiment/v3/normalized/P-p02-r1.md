## angles
- Hospitals renovate occupied wards by "decanting" — moving patients unit by unit into swing space, renovating the empty unit, then reversing the flow. -> Local-PV pinned stateful services need a compatible bed (node) prepared first; the real constraint is "where does workload X live while its node gets replaced." -> Build one small "swing" node group on the new version first, migrate the least-risky stateful service into it as a dry run, then use that same swing group as the landing zone for the next one, rolling capacity through the fleet instead of upgrading in place; also gives a rollback bed if migration goes wrong.
- Airlines maintain an MEL — a pre-agreed list of what's allowed to be broken and what grounds the plane. The webhook outage was a single unlisted item taking down the whole fleet because nothing gated it. -> The 40-minute outage wasn't really an upgrade problem, it was a missing circuit-breaker problem — one webhook had blast radius over all pod scheduling. This is a systemic gap independent of version skew, and it's cheap to fix now versus during crunch week. -> Before touching versions, add failure-mode isolation — failurePolicy: Ignore (or scoped namespaceSelectors) on every admission webhook, so a bad webhook degrades gracefully instead of halting scheduling cluster-wide. One change de-risks every future upgrade, not just this one.
- When a company can't pay off debt by maturity date, the immediate move often isn't "faster repayment," it's negotiating an extension or bridge loan to convert a hard deadline into a soft one. -> AWS sells Extended Support for EKS control planes past standard end-of-support. Ten weeks with 8 teams at low capacity is a classic "bridge financing" situation, not a "sprint harder" situation. -> Price out Extended Support for the oldest cluster as explicit fallback/insurance and communicate upward as "the deadline is soft if we choose to pay for it" — this reframes from cliff-edge deadline to cost-vs-speed tradeoff you control, which usually changes how much cooperation you get from the eight teams.
- Forestry crews don't fight wildfires by improvising containment lines in the middle of them — they pre-cut firebreaks and run small controlled burns in calm conditions specifically to learn how fuel behaves before the uncontrolled version arrives. -> You have no staging cluster that mirrors production, which means the first real signal about deprecated-API breakage or webhook behavior under the new version is production itself. -> Rather than building a full staging mirror (expensive, tight timeline), carve a small low-traffic node group inside one existing cluster, upgrade only that group, and cut over 2-3 of the least critical of the ~60 workloads to run live traffic there for a week. It's a controlled burn at survivable scale that surfaces admission-webhook and API-deprecation surprises before they touch the other 55+ workloads.
